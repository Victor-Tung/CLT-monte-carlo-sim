Project 1 - Final Summary

Overall Research Question

The goal of this project was to investigate how quickly the sampling distribution of the sample mean becomes approximately normal for different types of populations. The project also examined what happens when the assumptions of the usual Central Limit Theorem are questionable or fail.

How Approximate Normality Was Judged

I used several forms of evidence rather than relying on one rule or only visually inspecting the graphs. For each population, I generated 10,000 repeated samples at several sample sizes and calculated the sample mean for each repetition. I evaluated approximate normality using histograms, normal Q-Q plots, skewness, excess kurtosis, simulated and theoretical means, simulated and theoretical standard errors, and the SE ratio. I selected the smallest sample size for which the graphical and numerical evidence were reasonably consistent with approximate normality. The SE ratio assessed whether the spread of the simulated sample means agreed with theory, while the graphical diagnostics and shape statistics provided evidence about normality.

Is n = 30 a Universal Rule?

The final results do not support treating (n = 30) as a universal guarantee. It is a useful rule of thumb for several finite-mean and finite-variance populations, but the speed of convergence depends on the original population. The standard normal population was approximately normal at (n = 1), while the die, exponential, small binomial, and custom life/death populations showed stronger evidence of approximate normality at larger sample sizes. The dependent machine-failure process showed the strongest evidence at (n = 200) because the observations were not independent. The Cauchy case was an important exception because increasing (n) did not overcome the lack of a finite mean and variance.

Which Populations Converged Fastest and Slowest?

The standard normal population converged immediately because it was already normally distributed. The six-sided die population also converged relatively quickly because it was symmetric and bounded, although it remained discrete at smaller sample sizes. The exponential and small binomial populations converged more gradually because they were right-skewed. The custom life/death population showed stronger evidence of approximate normality around n = 30 because it had an irregular and asymmetric shape. The dependent machine-failure process converged more slowly because observations influenced one another. The Cauchy population did not converge to an ordinary normal sampling distribution because extreme values continued to affect the sample mean. Within the displayed range, its histograms remained broadly similar across sample sizes, while the numerical results showed that its mean and tail behavior remained unstable.

What Happened When CLT Assumptions Were Violated?

The Cauchy experiment showed that simply increasing the sample size does not always make sample averages look normally distributed. Because the Cauchy distribution can produce extremely large values, a few unusual observations can continue to strongly affect the average, even when many observations are included. The dependent machine-failure experiment showed that observations cannot always be treated as unrelated because one failure time can influence the next failure time. This dependence slows the process of becoming approximately normal and makes the usual independent-observation reasoning less direct. The non-identical Bernoulli experiment showed that observations do not always need to follow exactly the same distribution. However, when the probability of success changes from one observation to the next, those changing probabilities must be included when calculating the expected variation in the sample averages. Together, these examples show that the Central Limit Theorem is useful, but its conclusions depend on the type of data being analyzed and the assumptions made about the observations.

Reproducibility

The simulations use a seed based on the last four digits of my student ID so that the random results can be reproduced. Using a seed does not prevent unusual results or guarantee that the simulation is unbiased. Instead, it ensures that the same code produces the same random samples each time it is run. When one experiment is rerun with a different seed, the exact simulated means, standard errors, and shape statistics may change slightly, but the overall conclusions should remain similar. This shows that the conclusions are based on the general simulation pattern rather than one particular random sample.

Final Takeaway

The simulations showed that there is no universal sample size that guarantees an approximately normal sampling distribution. For each population, I generated repeated samples at several sample sizes and evaluated the resulting sample means using graphical and numerical evidence. The standard normal population was approximately normal immediately at (n=1). The six-sided die, exponential, small binomial, and custom life/death populations showed stronger evidence of approximate normality around (n=30), which I selected as a defensible cutoff rather than a universal rule.

The most surprising result was that increasing the sample size did not stabilize the Cauchy sampling distribution because the Cauchy population has no finite ordinary mean or variance. The dependent machine-failure process also converged more slowly because observations influenced one another, with (n=200) providing the strongest evidence of approximate normality. In contrast, the non-identical Bernoulli process became approximately normal around (n=30) when the changing success probabilities were correctly included in the theoretical variance and standard-error calculations.

Overall, the results demonstrate that the appropriate sample size depends on the population’s shape, variability, and dependence structure. Therefore, researchers should evaluate the observed sampling distribution using simulations, graphical diagnostics, skewness, kurtosis, and standard-error comparisons instead of automatically assuming that (n=30) will always be sufficient.
